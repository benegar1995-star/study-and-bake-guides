How to Sell Holiday Gift Certificates From Your Home Bakery

By mid-December most home bakers are fully booked. Gift certificates let you keep selling without adding a single extra bake to the busiest weeks of the year. Customers get a last-minute gift that's personal and local, and you get new customers in January and February, when orders are usually slow.

They do need clear rules and careful records. A gift certificate is a promise to bake something later, so you need to know exactly which ones are out there and how much each one is still worth. Here's how to set it up.

Step 1: Check the rules before you print anything

A few things to look up first (this is general information, not legal or tax advice):

  • Expiration dates. In the U.S., federal law generally doesn't allow store gift certificates and gift cards to expire sooner than five years after they're sold, and many states have stricter rules. Some ban expiration dates or fees completely. The simplest choice is to print "No expiration."
  • Promotional or bonus certificates (like "get a $10 bonus card when you buy $50") are often treated differently from ones a customer paid for. Label them clearly as promotional and print their terms.
  • Cottage food rules. Whatever the customer redeems the certificate for still has to be an item you're allowed to sell, labeled and handed over the way your state allows. Some states require in-person sales or limit delivery. If you need a refresher on labels, see our cottage food label basics.
  • Income and sales tax. Money from a gift certificate usually isn't counted as a sale until it's redeemed, and where sales tax applies it's often collected at redemption. Ask your tax preparer how to record it in your books.

Step 2: Decide your rules

Write these down once, then print a short version on a terms card that goes with every certificate:

  • Amounts. Offer a few fixed amounts ($25, $50, $75) and maybe a custom amount above a minimum. Or sell "good for one..." certificates for a specific item, such as a dozen decorated cookies, a 6-inch cake or a holiday pie. Item certificates are easy to give and easy to plan for.
  • What it can buy. Anything on your menu, or only certain items?
  • How to order with one. Custom cakes need notice. Say how many days ahead certificate holders should order, and any dates you're not taking redemptions (Valentine's week, for example).
  • Partial use. If someone spends $32 of a $50 certificate, write the $18 balance on the certificate and in your log.
  • Lost certificates. Will you replace one if the number is in your log? Many small bakers do, which is one more reason to keep a good log.
  • Cash back. Most small businesses say "not redeemable for cash except where required by law." Some states require cash back for small leftover balances, so check yours.

Step 3: Number, sign and log every certificate

This is the part that protects you. Before a certificate leaves your hands:

  1. Give it a number. Start at 1001 so they look established.
  2. Sign or stamp it. Only numbered, signed certificates are valid.
  3. Log it immediately: number, date, buyer and their contact info, recipient, amount, and how they paid.

Your log is the real record. The paper certificate is just the customer's proof. When someone redeems it, check the number against your log, write the date and amount used, and update the balance. Mark fully used certificates "USED" and keep them. If you spoil a blank, write "VOID" on it and in the log. Store unused blanks somewhere safe.

At the end of each month, add up what's still outstanding: last month's balance, plus what you sold, minus what was redeemed. That's how much baking you still owe, and it's money you've already been paid for. Our guide to home bakery bookkeeping has more on keeping simple, tidy records.

Step 4: Make them look like a gift

A good-looking certificate sells itself. Print on heavy card stock, tuck it in an envelope with your logo sticker, or pair it with a small treat. "Gift certificate plus a box of four cookies to hand over on the day" is a great upsell, as long as the buyer pays for both. Wallet-size gift cards (3.5 by 2 inches) are handy for stocking stuffers and teacher gifts.

For last-minute buyers, you can email a photo or PDF of a numbered certificate. Log it the same way, and note that only the number in your log is valid.

Step 5: Promote them, with a clear last day

Keep the promotion short and simple:

  • Tell your regular customers first, by text or email.
  • Post on social media with a photo of a finished certificate in its envelope.
  • Put a small card in every holiday order and a sign on your market or pickup table.
  • Post a "last chance for holiday gifts" reminder a few days before your final day.

Pick a last day that leaves time for pickup or mailing. If you're also running holiday orders, our guides to holiday pre-orders and holiday cookie boxes cover caps and pickup planning.

Step 6: Plan for January and February

Most certificates are redeemed in the first months of the year. That's good for a slow season, but don't sell more than you can bake. Before you start, estimate how many might come in during January and February, decide on blackout dates, and make sure your ingredient costs are covered if every certificate is redeemed. Your full cost per item is your floor. Our guide on how to price baked goods shows how to work it out.

Quick checklist

  • Expiration, promotional and cottage food rules checked for my state
  • Amounts, partial use, lost-certificate and ordering rules written down
  • Terms card printed to go with every certificate
  • Every certificate numbered, signed and logged at the moment of sale
  • Redemptions logged with balances; used ones marked and kept
  • Monthly total of outstanding certificates
  • A clear last day to buy for the holidays
