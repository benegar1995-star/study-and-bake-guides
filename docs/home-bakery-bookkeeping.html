Simple Bookkeeping for a Home Bakery: Track Sales, Expenses, and Real Profit

Plenty of home bakers know exactly how many dozen cookies they sold last month but have no idea whether they made money. Money comes in through cash, cards, and payment apps. It goes out at the grocery store, the craft store, and online supply shops. Without a simple record, it all blurs together, until tax season arrives and you're digging through receipts.

Bookkeeping for a small home bakery doesn't need to be complicated. This guide gives you a lightweight system you can keep up with in a few minutes a week. It's general information, not tax advice. A tax professional can tell you what applies to your situation.

Why bother?

Good records help you:

  • Know your real profit, not just your sales
  • Price correctly, because you'll see what ingredients and packaging actually cost
  • Spot your best products and sales channels
  • Make tax time easier. In the US, income from selling baked goods is generally taxable, and expenses may be deductible.
  • Separate business from personal spending

Step 1: Separate your business money

If you can, use a separate bank account, or at least a separate card, for bakery purchases and income. It isn't strictly required for a small side business, but it makes record-keeping dramatically easier. When business and personal spending mix, you'll spend more time sorting receipts later.

Step 2: Record every sale

For each order, record:

  • Date
  • Customer (or "market sale" for walk-up purchases)
  • Product
  • Quantity and unit price
  • Total
  • Payment method: cash, card, payment app, check
  • Paid? This is useful for deposits and balances due.
  • Sales channel: word of mouth, social media, farmers market, website, repeat customer

Tracking the channel is underrated. After a few months, you'll know whether your weekend market or your social media posts are actually bringing in orders, and where to spend your energy. (If you're considering a market, see our guide to planning a farmers market booth.)

If you take deposits for custom orders, record the deposit when received and the balance when paid, or record the full order and mark the payment status.

Step 3: Record every expense, with a category

Keep every receipt, on paper or photographed, and log each expense:

  • Date
  • Vendor
  • Description
  • Category
  • Amount

Useful categories for a home bakery include:

  • Ingredients: flour, sugar, butter, chocolate, and so on
  • Packaging: boxes, liners, bags, labels, cake boards
  • Equipment: mixers, pans, piping tips, cooling racks
  • Marketing: business cards, website, ads, market booth fees
  • Permits and fees: cottage food registration, licenses, payment processing fees
  • Delivery and mileage: if you deliver, keep a mileage log with dates, destinations, and miles
  • Other

Categories show you where money goes. If packaging is quietly eating your margins, you'll see it.

Mixed-use purchases: if you buy groceries for both home and business, split the business portion out on the receipt. For larger questions, like home office or kitchen use, ask a tax professional, because rules are specific.

Step 4: Review monthly

Once a month, total up:

  • Number of orders
  • Revenue: total sales
  • Expenses by category
  • Profit: revenue minus expenses
  • Margin: profit divided by revenue

Then ask a few questions:

  • Which products sold best? Which made the most profit?
  • Did any expense category jump?
  • Is profit growing, flat, or shrinking?
  • Do prices need updating because ingredient costs rose?

A monthly review takes 15 to 30 minutes and is the single best habit for running a profitable bakery.

Step 5: Keep inventory in check

You don't need complicated inventory accounting for a small home bakery, but a simple stock list helps you avoid emergency store runs and waste. Before each baking day, check key ingredients and supplies against the minimum amounts you like to keep on hand, and build a shopping list from the gaps.

Step 6: Prepare for taxes throughout the year

General points many US home bakers should be aware of (confirm with a tax professional):

  • Income from a home bakery is generally reported on your tax return, and many sole proprietors report business income and expenses on Schedule C.
  • Self-employment tax may apply to net earnings above certain thresholds.
  • Payment platforms may send you tax forms for payments received, but you're responsible for reporting all business income, including cash.
  • Keep receipts and records for several years.
  • If your profit is significant, you may need to make estimated tax payments during the year.

Your sales and expense log, organized by category, is exactly what you or your tax preparer will need.

A weekly 10-minute routine

  1. Enter the week's sales from your order forms and payment apps.
  2. Photograph and enter receipts.
  3. Mark any outstanding balances.
  4. Glance at the month-to-date totals.

Then do the monthly review at the start of each new month.

Paper or spreadsheet?

Both work. Paper is easy to keep next to your order forms. A spreadsheet adds automatic totals and monthly summaries. Many bakers use both: paper for daily orders and a spreadsheet for the books.
