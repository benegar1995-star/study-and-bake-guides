Cottage Food Labels: What Home Bakers Usually Need (Check Your State)

If you sell baked goods from your home kitchen in the United States, you're probably operating under your state's cottage food law. These laws let people make and sell certain lower-risk foods from home, often with lighter requirements than a commercial kitchen. Almost all of them include labeling rules.

Important: cottage food laws are set by each state, and sometimes by counties or cities. They differ a lot: which foods are allowed, where you can sell, sales limits, registration or permit requirements, and exactly what your label must say. This guide describes common elements so you know what to look for. It is not legal advice. Always read your state's current rules, usually published by the state department of agriculture or health, and contact them if you're unsure.

Why labels matter

Labels do several jobs at once:

  • They tell customers what's in the food, which is critical for allergies.
  • They show who made it and how to reach you.
  • They disclose that the food was made in a home kitchen, which many states require.
  • They make your products look professional and memorable.

Elements commonly required

Many states' cottage food labeling rules include some or all of the following. Your state may require more, less, or specific wording.

1. Product name

The common name of the food, such as "Chocolate Chip Cookies" or "Cinnamon Swirl Bread." Be clear and accurate.

2. Producer name and address

Your name or business name and an address. Some states require your physical address, and others allow different contact details. Some require a registration or permit number. Check yours.

3. Home-kitchen statement

Many states require a specific statement saying the food was made in a home kitchen that isn't inspected by the state or local health department, or similar language. The exact wording often matters, and some states specify font size or placement. Copy your state's required wording exactly instead of paraphrasing.

4. Ingredients, in descending order by weight

List ingredients from the largest amount to the smallest. Sub-ingredients of components are often listed too. For example, chocolate chips might be followed by their own ingredients in parentheses. Check the labels of the ingredients you buy to build accurate lists.

5. Allergen information

Clear allergen labeling protects your customers and you. Federal law identifies major food allergens: milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans, and sesame. Sesame was added more recently, so make sure any old templates include it. Many cottage food rules require declaring these, either within the ingredient list or in a separate "Contains:" statement. Even where it isn't strictly required, it's good practice. If your kitchen also handles allergens that aren't in a product, consider whether and how to note cross-contact, according to your state's guidance.

6. Net quantity

The amount in the package, by weight, volume, or count, depending on your state's rules and the product. Use a kitchen scale and list the weight in the units your state expects. Many labels show both US and metric units.

7. Other items some states require

  • Registration or permit number
  • Date made or "best by" date
  • A statement that the product isn't for resale
  • Nutrition labeling, usually only if you make health or nutrient claims, but check your state

Things to avoid

  • Health claims such as "healthy," "low sugar," or "gluten-free." These can trigger additional rules. "Gluten-free" in particular has a specific federal meaning. Avoid such claims unless you've confirmed you can meet the requirements.
  • Selling foods your state doesn't allow under cottage food rules. Many states exclude items that need refrigeration, such as cream-filled pastries, cheesecakes, or some frostings. Check the approved list.
  • Copying another baker's label wording without checking that it matches your state's rules.

Practical label tips

  • Keep a master ingredient list for each recipe, and update it whenever you change a brand or ingredient.
  • Use readable fonts. Tiny decorative fonts may be pretty, but legibility comes first. Some states set minimum sizes.
  • Test print on plain paper and hold it behind your label sheet to check alignment before printing on labels.
  • Choose label sizes that fit your packaging. Full ingredient labels need more space than decorative stickers.
  • Keep records. Note which recipe version and ingredient brands went into which batches. It helps if a customer has an allergy question.

Example label structure

Here is an illustrative layout. Fill in your own details and your state's required wording.

PRODUCT: Brown Butter Chocolate Chip Cookies (6 pack) Ingredients: Wheat flour, semi-sweet chocolate chips (sugar, chocolate liquor, cocoa butter, soy lecithin, vanilla), butter (milk), brown sugar, sugar, eggs, vanilla extract, baking soda, salt. Contains: Wheat, Milk, Egg, Soy. Net wt: 12 oz (340 g) Made by: [Your name / business], [address as required] [Your state's required home-kitchen statement] Baked on: [date]

This example is for format only. Your ingredients, allergens, and required statements will differ.

Where to find your state's rules

Search for "[your state] cottage food law" and look for official sources: your state department of agriculture, department of health, or a state extension service (often run by a public university). Extension services frequently publish plain-language guides. Make sure you're reading a current version, since these laws are updated from time to time.
