How to Ask for a Letter of Recommendation (Timeline, Brag Sheet, and Email Template)

A recommendation letter is one of the few parts of a college or scholarship application that you don't write yourself. That can feel like a loss of control, but you have more influence than you might think. Who you ask, when you ask, and what you hand them all shape how specific and convincing the letter turns out to be.

This guide covers the whole process: choosing recommenders, a realistic timeline, the "brag sheet" that makes a letter easy to write, and wording you can adapt for the request, the reminder, and the thank-you.

First, check what each application actually asks for

Requirements vary a lot, so read each one before you ask anyone:

  • How many letters, and from whom. Many colleges ask for a school counselor recommendation plus one or two teacher letters. Some scholarships want a letter from a community leader, employer, or coach instead of a teacher. Some don't want letters at all.
  • How letters are submitted. Often the recommender uploads directly to an application portal after you enter their email address. Others want a sealed letter, an email to a specific address, or a letter you upload yourself.
  • Deadline. Note whether the letter is due on the same date as your application or later.
  • Any form or questions the recommender must answer, such as rating scales or specific prompts.

Write all of this down next to each application. If one teacher is writing for several, a single list of deadlines and submission methods saves them real hassle.

Choosing the right people

The best recommender is someone who knows you well and can describe specific things you did, not simply the most impressive title you can find. A detailed letter from a teacher whose class you worked hard in usually beats a vague letter from someone important who barely knows you.

Good candidates include:

  • Teachers from core academic classes, ideally recent ones (for high school seniors, often junior-year or current senior-year teachers). Check whether a college wants teachers from particular subjects.
  • Your school counselor, who often writes a school-level recommendation for colleges as part of their normal process. Find out how your school handles counselor requests; many schools have their own forms or deadlines.
  • Employers or supervisors, especially useful for scholarships that value work ethic or responsibility.
  • Coaches, club advisors, or volunteer coordinators, when the scholarship focuses on leadership or service.

Avoid asking family members unless an application allows it, and match the recommender to the award: an engineering scholarship suits your math or science teacher, a service award suits your volunteer supervisor.

A realistic timeline

Teachers and counselors get many requests in the fall, and rushed letters tend to be generic, so give them time.

  • As early as possible (often spring of junior year for college applications): ask teachers whether they'd be willing to write for you in the fall. Some teachers cap how many letters they'll write, and early askers get the yes.
  • At least three to four weeks before the deadline: make the formal request, with your brag sheet and a list of deadlines. More notice is better in October and November.
  • About one week before the deadline: send one polite reminder if the letter hasn't been submitted.
  • Within a few days of submission: send a thank-you note.
  • When results arrive: let them know how it turned out.

If a deadline is less than three weeks away, you can still ask, but acknowledge the short notice and accept a "no" gracefully.

Ask in person first, then follow up in writing

Whenever possible, ask face to face. It's more personal and lets the recommender ask questions. Keep it simple: "Would you feel comfortable writing a strong recommendation letter for my scholarship and college applications this fall?"

That wording gives them a polite way to decline if they don't know you well enough. A lukewarm letter can hurt more than it helps, so an early "no" is useful information.

Then send a written follow-up with all the details in one message.

Build a brag sheet

A brag sheet is a one- or two-page summary that gives your recommender concrete material to work with. It's hard to remember specific details about one student among many, so this helps. Include:

  • Your full name, grade, and the names of the programs or scholarships you're applying for
  • A short list of your deadlines and how each letter should be submitted
  • Your academic interests and intended major or career, if you know it
  • Two or three specific moments from their class, team, or workplace: a project you're proud of, a time you improved after a setback, a contribution to discussions
  • Activities, jobs, and responsibilities outside school (including family responsibilities, which matter and are often overlooked)
  • Awards or recognition, if any
  • What you'd like them to highlight, for example your persistence, curiosity, or leadership. They may not use it, but it helps them choose.

Some schools provide their own brag sheet or "student questionnaire." If yours does, use it, and attach extra notes if you need more room.

Email template: the written request

Adapt this, keep it short, and attach your brag sheet:

Subject: Recommendation letter request: [Your name], [scholarship/college] deadlines

Dear [Mr./Ms./Dr. Last name],

Thank you again for agreeing to write a recommendation letter for me. As promised, here are the details in one place.

  • [Scholarship or college 1]: due [date], submitted by [portal invitation to your email / emailed to address / sealed letter]
  • [Scholarship or college 2]: due [date], submitted by [method]

I've attached a brag sheet with my activities and goals. I'd be especially grateful if you could mention [one or two specific things, such as the research project in your class or how I improved in the second semester].

Please let me know if you need anything else from me. I really appreciate your time.

Sincerely,
[Your full name]
[Grade / school] | [Phone or email]

If you're using an online portal, tell them to expect an automated invitation, and enter their email address carefully.

Template: the polite reminder

Hi [Mr./Ms./Dr. Last name], just a friendly reminder that the [scholarship/college] recommendation is due on [date]. Thank you so much for your help. Please let me know if there's anything else you need from me.

Send one reminder about a week out. If a portal shows the letter as submitted, skip it.

Template: the thank-you note

A handwritten card is a nice touch, but a sincere email is fine too:

Dear [Name], thank you for taking the time to write my recommendation for [scholarship/college]. I know how busy this time of year is, and I really appreciate your support. I'll let you know how it turns out.

Then tell them the result, win or lose. Recommenders who know their effort mattered are happy to write again, and you may need them for future scholarships, internships, or jobs.

A note on FERPA waivers and confidentiality

The Common App and some other systems ask whether you waive your right under FERPA (a federal student privacy law) to see your recommendation letters later. Many counselors suggest waiving, because colleges may view confidential letters as more candid, but it's your decision, so ask your counselor if you're unsure. Don't ask to read or edit your letter unless the recommender offers.

Keep track of every letter

Once you have several recommenders writing for several applications, letters become easy to lose track of. For each one, record the recommender's name and contact details, the date you asked, which applications the letter is for, each due date, whether it's been received, and whether you've sent a thank-you. Quick checklist

  • Check each application's letter requirements and submission method
  • Ask recommenders who know you well, early and in person if possible
  • Send one written request with deadlines, methods, and a brag sheet
  • Remind once, about a week before the deadline
  • Say thank you, and share the outcome later

Requirements and processes vary by school and scholarship, so always follow the specific instructions from each application and your school counseling office.
