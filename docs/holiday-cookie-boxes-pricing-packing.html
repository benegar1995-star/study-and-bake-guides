How to Price, Plan and Pack Holiday Cookie Boxes for a Home Bakery

Holiday cookie boxes are one of the best seasonal products a home bakery can sell. People buy them for family gatherings, teachers, neighbors and clients, and a beautiful box sells itself in photos. They're also easy to underprice. A dozen cookies looks like "a few dollars of butter and sugar" until you add the box, the liners, the ribbon, and the hours of decorating and packing.

Here's how to plan cookie boxes that customers love and that actually pay you for your time.

Step 1: Check your state's rules first

If you sell under your state's cottage food law, confirm that cookies are an allowed item (they usually are), what has to go on the label, and how you're allowed to sell and deliver. Some states limit total yearly sales or don't allow shipping. Every box will need a label, so plan for it now. Our cottage food label guide explains the usual parts, and our printable bakery labels give you a place to write them in.

Step 2: Keep the lineup small

Offer two or three box sizes at most, for example a 6-cookie gift box, a 12-cookie classic box and a 24-cookie party box. For each one, decide:

  • How many cookies and how many varieties
  • The price
  • A cap: the most boxes you'll sell
  • An order cutoff date

The cap matters most. Work out how many boxes you can really bake, decorate and pack in the time you have, then take off 10-15% for things going wrong. Our guide to holiday pre-orders explains caps, deposits and pickup slots in detail.

Step 3: Choose cookies that work together

A good box usually has three to five varieties with different shapes, colors and textures: maybe one decorated sugar cookie, one chocolate, one spiced and one buttery shortbread. When you choose, think about:

  • Shelf life. Pick cookies that stay good for about the same number of days, so the whole box can have one best-by date.
  • Texture. Crisp and soft cookies shouldn't touch. Soft cookies release moisture and make crisp ones go soft.
  • Strong flavors. Peppermint, anise and some spices can flavor everything near them. Wrap them on their own.
  • Fragility. Thin, sticky or very delicate cookies break and smear. Use only one, if any.
  • Allergens. Fewer allergens make labels simpler. Write down every allergen in every cookie.

Step 4: Price each box from its full cost

Price the box, not just the cookies. Add up:

  1. Cookies: the ingredient cost of each cookie, times how many are in the box
  2. Box or tin
  3. Liners, cups and dividers
  4. Ribbon, tag, label and sticker
  5. Labor: the minutes per box for mixing, baking, decorating and packing, times your hourly rate
  6. Overhead: a share of utilities, equipment, card fees and supplies (many bakers use about 10%)

Then add your margin: price = full cost ÷ (1 − margin).

Here's an example for a 12-cookie box with four varieties:

  • Cookies: $2.85 (12 cookies at $0.20-$0.28 each)
  • Box: $1.60, liners: $0.40, ribbon and tag: $0.50
  • Labor: 25 minutes at $20/hour = $8.33
  • Overhead: 10% of the cookie and packaging costs = $0.54
  • Full cost: $14.22

At a 30% margin, $14.22 ÷ 0.70 = $20.31, so this baker would price the box at $21, for a profit of about $6.78 per box. These numbers are only an illustration. Use your own ingredient costs, packaging and hourly rate. Our guide on how to price baked goods explains each part, and our Recipe Cost Calculator works out the cost per cookie from your recipes.

Notice that labor is the biggest cost. That's normal for decorated cookies, and it's why "just a few dollars" pricing doesn't work.

Step 5: Bake and freeze on a schedule

Work backward from your first pickup day. Many cookie doughs freeze well, either as dough or as balls or logs ready to bake, and plenty of baked cookies freeze well before decorating. Test your own recipes first, because some cookies don't come back well.

A typical plan:

  • 2-3 weeks out: order packaging; make and freeze doughs
  • 1 week out: bake cookies that keep the longest
  • 3-4 days out: bake and decorate decorated cookies (royal icing needs drying time)
  • 1-2 days out: bake the most delicate cookies, then pack
  • Pickup day: final check, labels and handoff

Keep a test cookie of each kind and taste it on day 3, 5, 7 and 10. Use the shortest "still great" result of any cookie in the box as the box's best-by date. That's more honest than guessing.

Step 6: Pack for freshness and looks

  • Let cookies cool completely before packing. Warm cookies sweat and go soft.
  • Use food-safe liners, cups or bags against the cookies.
  • Put sturdy cookies on the bottom and around the edges, and decorated cookies on top.
  • Fill gaps with crinkle paper so cookies don't slide.
  • Heat-seal or tie bags so the box stays airtight.
  • Put your label on every box, plus a small card with storage tips and how to order again.

Before the season, pack one test box and shake it gently. If cookies break, add dividers or more filler.

Step 7: Take orders the simple way

Use one order form for every order, whether it comes by text, DM, phone or in person. Write down the box size and quantity, the pickup date and time, allergies, any gift message, the deposit and the balance due. Write your policies on the form too: the order cutoff, the deposit amount, and a note if your kitchen also handles common allergens.

At the cutoff, total the boxes by size. That tells you how much packaging to buy and, multiplied by cookies per box, how many of each cookie to bake. Record your sales and costs in your regular books. Our home bakery bookkeeping guide explains a simple routine.
