How to Price Baked Goods for a Home Bakery (Cost, Labor, and Profit)

"How much should I charge?" is the question almost every home baker struggles with. Charge too little and you'll work long weekends for almost nothing, or even lose money once you count everything. Charge too much without a clear reason and you may worry customers will walk away. The way out is to stop guessing and price from your actual costs, then adjust for your market.

This guide walks through a practical pricing method you can use for cookies, cupcakes, bread, or custom cakes.

The pricing formula

At its core, a sustainable price covers four things:

  1. Ingredients: what goes into the recipe
  2. Packaging: boxes, liners, bags, labels, cake boards, ribbon
  3. Overhead: utilities, equipment wear, small supplies, fees
  4. Labor: paying yourself for your time

Add those up to get your true cost. Then add a profit margin on top, money for the business beyond just paying you, so you can replace a mixer, try a new product, or grow.

Step 1: Calculate ingredient cost per recipe

This step surprises most bakers, because a recipe's real cost is often higher, or lower, than they assumed.

  1. List every ingredient you buy with its package size and price. For example, a bag of flour of a certain weight at a certain price.
  2. Calculate cost per unit: package price ÷ package size. This gives you a cost per gram, ounce, milliliter, or egg.
  3. For each recipe, multiply the amount used by the cost per unit for each ingredient.
  4. Add them up for the recipe's total ingredient cost.

Use consistent units. If your price list uses grams, measure the recipe in grams. Weighing ingredients makes costing much more accurate than cups, because a "cup of flour" varies a lot depending on how it's scooped.

Don't forget small things: vanilla, food coloring, sprinkles, parchment, and the butter used to grease pans. They add up, especially premium items like real vanilla, chocolate, and nuts.

Update ingredient prices every few months, or whenever you notice a big change at the store.

Step 2: Add packaging

Count everything that leaves with the customer: cupcake liners, boxes, inserts, cake boards, bags, twist ties, stickers, labels, ribbon. Divide bulk-pack prices by the number of units to get a per-order cost. For custom cakes, a sturdy board and box can be a meaningful part of the cost.

Step 3: Account for overhead

Overhead is harder to measure, but ignoring it means you're subsidizing your customers. It includes:

  • Electricity or gas for your oven and mixer
  • Wear and eventual replacement of pans, mixers, and tools
  • Cleaning supplies
  • Any permits, registration, or inspection fees required where you live
  • Payment processing fees, if you accept cards or payment apps
  • Website or marketing costs

A simple approach is to add a percentage on top of ingredient cost to cover overhead. Many bakers start somewhere around 10%, then refine it once they've tracked a few months of actual expenses. If you track your monthly business expenses (see our bookkeeping guide), you can calculate a more accurate figure.

Step 4: Pay yourself for labor

This is where many home bakers undercharge the most. Track the real time an order takes, including:

  • Shopping for ingredients
  • Baking and cooling
  • Decorating
  • Packaging
  • Communicating with the customer
  • Cleaning up
  • Delivery or pickup coordination

Then multiply by an hourly wage you'd consider fair for skilled work. If a batch of decorated cupcakes takes two hours start to finish, those two hours belong in the price. Leaving labor out turns your business into an expensive hobby.

Step 5: Add a profit margin

Once you have total cost (ingredients + packaging + overhead + labor), add profit. A useful way to think about it:

Price = total cost ÷ (1 − target margin)

If your total cost per unit is a certain amount and you want a 30% margin, divide the cost by 0.70. This gives you a price where 30% of each sale is profit after all costs. Using margin this way, instead of simply adding 30% to cost, makes sure the profit share is what you intended.

If you sell through a platform or take cards, consider building those fees in too, either in overhead or as a separate buffer.

Step 6: Compare to your market, then decide

Your cost-based price is your floor: the lowest price that keeps the business sustainable. Now look at your market:

  • What do other home bakers and local bakeries near you charge for similar items?
  • Is your product similar, or does it stand out in design, flavor, dietary options, or presentation?
  • Who are your customers? Everyday treats and custom celebration cakes are priced very differently.

If your cost-based price is well below the market, you may be able to charge more. If it's well above, look for efficiencies (bigger batches, simpler designs, bulk ingredient purchases) or focus on customers who value what you offer. Avoid simply dropping below cost to match a competitor.

Pricing custom cakes

Custom cakes are more variable, so many bakers use:

  • A base price per serving for a standard flavor and simple finish
  • Add-ons for premium flavors or fillings, fondant, sugar flowers, tiers, toppers, or complex designs
  • Delivery and setup fees based on distance
  • A minimum order amount

Collect details on a written order form (flavor, servings, design, date, allergies) so your quote reflects what you'll actually make. Most custom bakers also take a deposit to hold the date.

Raising prices without drama

Costs rise. When they do:

  • Give regular customers a heads-up.
  • Update prices at a natural point, like the new year or a new menu.
  • Explain briefly if you like, but you don't owe anyone a detailed justification.
  • Focus on quality and service. Customers who value your work will usually stay.
