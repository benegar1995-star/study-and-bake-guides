How to Plan a Farmers Market Booth for a Home Bakery (Rules, Setup, and What to Bring)

A farmers market can be a great fit for a home bakery. Shoppers are already in a mood to buy local food, you get instant feedback on new flavors, and a regular booth turns first-time buyers into custom-order customers. But market days also involve early mornings, weather, rules you may not have dealt with before, and a lot of guesswork about how much to bake.

This guide walks through the planning in order, from checking whether you're allowed to sell at a market to reviewing your numbers afterward.

Step 1: Check your state's cottage food rules first

Cottage food laws differ a lot from state to state, and they directly affect market selling. Before you apply anywhere, find out:

  • Whether market sales are allowed. Most states allow direct, in-person sales, and farmers markets are commonly included, but the details vary. Some states also limit sales by venue, county, or annual revenue.
  • Whether you need a permit, license, or registration, and whether it must be displayed at your booth.
  • Which foods are allowed. Many states limit cottage food to items that don't need refrigeration, so things like cream cheese frosting, custards, or cream fillings may not qualify. Check before you plan your menu.
  • Labeling requirements for packaged items. Our cottage food label guide covers what labels commonly include.
  • Rules for free samples, which are sometimes treated differently from packaged sales.

The best sources are your state's own agency, usually the department of agriculture or department of health, and your local or county health department. Read the rules on their official websites or call them. Market managers often know the local requirements too, but the agency is the authority.

Also check how sales tax applies to your products. Treatment of baked goods varies by state and sometimes by how the item is sold (for example, packaged to go versus eaten on site). Your state's department of revenue is the place to confirm whether you need to register and collect tax. This guide is general information, not legal or tax advice.

Step 2: Choose a market and apply

Markets differ in size, foot traffic, rules, and fees. Visit a few as a shopper before applying, and note:

  • How busy it is, and at what times
  • How many other bakers there are, and what they sell and charge
  • Who shops there: families, commuters, tourists, regulars with tote bags
  • What the booths look like: tents, tables, signs

When you apply, expect to be asked for some combination of: a vendor application, a booth fee (per day or per season), a copy of your cottage food permit or registration if your state requires one, and sometimes proof of liability insurance. Requirements are set by each market, so read the vendor rules carefully. Many markets have rules about tent weights, arrival and teardown times, and whether you can leave early.

Record every booth fee and insurance cost as a business expense on the day you pay it.

Step 3: Plan a focused menu

A market booth works best with a small, clear menu rather than everything you can make. Aim for:

  • A few bestsellers that travel well and hold up for several hours outdoors: cookies, brownies, bars, quick breads, and many yeast breads tend to do well
  • One or two "impulse" items at a lower price point, such as a single cookie or mini loaf
  • One higher-priced item for people buying for a gathering, such as a dozen box or a whole loaf
  • Something seasonal to give regulars a reason to come back

Avoid items that melt, wilt, or need refrigeration you can't provide (and may not be allowed anyway). Package or wrap items in a way your state's rules accept, and label them if required.

Step 4: Decide how much to bake

There's no formula that works for every market. For your first one, it's reasonable to make a conservative estimate, accept that you might sell out or have leftovers, and treat the day as data collection.

Then track, for each item:

  • How many you brought
  • How many you sold
  • How many were left over
  • What time you sold out, if you did

Selling out by mid-morning means you could have sold more. Lots of leftovers means scale back or rethink that item. After a few markets, your own numbers will be far more reliable than any rule of thumb. Plan ahead for leftovers too: whether you'll freeze, discount, or donate them, within your local rules.

Step 5: Price for the booth, not just the kitchen

Start from your real costs. Our guide to pricing baked goods walks through ingredients, packaging, overhead, labor, and profit. For market sales, also count booth fees, card processing fees, and the extra hours of setup, selling, and teardown.

A few market-specific tips:

  • Round prices to make cash transactions quick, such as $3, $5, or $12.
  • Offer a simple bundle, like "3 for $10" or a half-dozen box, to raise the average sale.
  • Make every price visible. Many shoppers won't ask, and unlabeled items often get passed by.

Step 6: Set up a booth that sells

You don't need an expensive setup, but it should look clean, organized, and easy to understand from a few steps away:

  • A large, readable sign with your bakery name
  • A clear menu board or price signs for every item
  • Height and levels using crates, risers, or cake stands so products aren't all flat on the table
  • A tablecloth that reaches the ground, which hides your storage bins underneath
  • Covered displays to protect food from dust, insects, and handling, as local rules may require
  • Business cards or a small sign about custom orders, so market shoppers can find you later

Do a practice setup at home. It shows you what fits on your table and how long setup really takes.

Step 7: Plan for payments

Most shoppers expect to pay by card or payment app, but some prefer cash.

  • Card reader or app: test it beforehand and know what happens if the signal is weak. Some readers can take payments offline and process them later; check how yours works and what the risks are.
  • Cash float: bring enough small bills and coins to make change. Count it before you leave and write down the starting amount.
  • Record the day's sales that evening while it's fresh: totals by payment method, items sold, and the booth fee.

Market day packing list

  • Permit, registration, or other documents your state or market requires
  • Tent, tent weights, table(s), chairs, tablecloth
  • Signs, price cards, menu board, tape or clips
  • Products, packed so they won't crush, plus extra packaging and bags
  • Display stands, covers, and serving tongs or gloves
  • Card reader (charged), backup battery, phone
  • Cash box with starting float
  • Hand sanitizer, paper towels, trash bags
  • Water, snacks, sunscreen, and layers for the weather
  • Inventory sheet and pen

After the market: a 15-minute review

The same evening, write down:

  • Total sales, split by cash and card
  • Units sold and left over for each item
  • Booth fee and any other costs for the day
  • Weather and foot traffic (a rainy day isn't a fair test of a new product)
  • What customers asked for that you didn't have
  • One thing to change next time

Logging market days alongside your other sales shows whether the market is actually profitable once fees and hours are counted, and how it compares to custom orders.
