FAFSA and CSS Profile Checklist: What to Gather Before You Start

Most of the stress around financial aid forms comes from hunting for documents halfway through, not from the questions themselves. If you gather everything first, the FAFSA often takes well under an hour, and the CSS Profile goes much faster too.

This guide covers what to set up, what to collect for each person, how to build a deadline list, and what happens after you hit submit. Dates, fees and rules change every year, so check the official sites (StudentAid.gov for the FAFSA, College Board for the CSS Profile) and each school's financial aid page before you rely on any detail.

Step 1: Know which forms you need

The FAFSA (Free Application for Federal Student Aid) is free and is used for federal grants, loans and work-study. Most states and many colleges use it for their own aid too. For the 2027-28 school year, the form opened in late September 2026 and asks about 2025 income, the "prior-prior" tax year.

The CSS Profile is run by College Board and is required by a few hundred colleges and scholarship programs, mostly private ones, for their own institutional aid. It asks more detailed questions (home equity, business assets, and sometimes a noncustodial parent's finances). There is usually a fee per school, with fee waivers for many lower- and middle-income families. Check the College Board site for this year's amounts.

Open each college's financial aid page and write down which forms it requires. Many schools only need the FAFSA. If none of your schools list the CSS Profile, skip it.

Step 2: Figure out who the "contributors" are

On the FAFSA, anyone who has to provide information is called a contributor. For a dependent student, that's usually one or two parents. For a married independent student, it may be a spouse. The form's questions decide who counts, so answer them honestly rather than guessing.

Each contributor needs:

  • Their own StudentAid.gov account (username and password), created with their own email
  • To complete their own section of the form
  • To give consent for their federal tax information to be transferred from the IRS. If a required contributor doesn't consent, the student won't be eligible for federal aid, so make sure everyone knows this step matters.

Make the accounts a week or two early. Identity checks can take a few days. Keep a simple list of who has made an account, who has been invited, and who has finished their part. Never write passwords or full Social Security numbers on a printed checklist.

Step 3: Gather documents for each person

Use the same list for the student and for each contributor. Not everything will apply to everyone.

  • Social Security number (or a note if a contributor doesn't have one)
  • Federal tax return for the required year (for 2027-28, the 2025 return), with schedules
  • W-2s and other income records for that year, in case you need to check numbers
  • Records of untaxed income, such as child support received
  • Current balances of cash, savings and checking accounts (as of the day you file)
  • Current value of investments, not counting retirement accounts or the home you live in
  • Business or farm records, if anyone owns one
  • Foreign income or foreign tax return, if anyone filed abroad
  • Permanent resident card or A-number, if the student isn't a U.S. citizen
  • Family size and how many family members will be in college
  • State of legal residence, and the date it started if it's recent
  • Your list of colleges (each one has a federal school code)

For the CSS Profile, add: current home value and amount owed, other real estate, business and farm values, the year's expected income, and the noncustodial parent's details if any of your schools require them. Have the CSS codes for your colleges ready too.

Step 4: Build one deadline list for every school

This is where families most often lose money. Aid deadlines are not the same as admission deadlines, and they vary from school to school. For each college, write down:

  • The FAFSA priority date (filing after it can mean less aid, even if the "deadline" is later)
  • The CSS Profile deadline, if required
  • Any document deadline, such as tax returns uploaded through IDOC
  • Whether the school has its own extra aid form

Then add your state's aid deadline. Some state grants are first-come, first-served, so filing early can matter even when the official deadline is months away. Use the earliest date that applies to you as your real deadline.

Step 5: After you submit

Submitting isn't the end. Watch for three things:

  1. The FAFSA Submission Summary. Read it carefully and fix any errors through StudentAid.gov.
  2. Verification. Some students are selected to confirm their information. The school will tell you what to send. Respond quickly, because aid can't be finalized until you do.
  3. Document requests. CSS Profile schools often request tax documents through IDOC, by email. Upload them by the earliest school deadline.

Keep a simple log of every request: who asked, what they need, the due date, and when you sent it. Check your email (and spam folder) and each school's student portal every week or so.

If your family's finances changed a lot after the tax year on the form (job loss, medical bills, divorce), contact each aid office and ask about a special circumstances review. Write down who you spoke with and what they asked for.
