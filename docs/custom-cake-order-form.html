What to Include on a Custom Cake Order Form (and Why Each Part Matters)

Custom cake orders are where small details cause big problems. A customer remembers asking for a lemon filling, but you wrote down raspberry. Someone says "about 30 people" but expects tall slices for 40. Pickup was "Saturday," but which time? A clear, consistent order form prevents most of these misunderstandings. It also makes you look more professional and helps you quote accurately.

Here's what to include on a custom cake order form, and why each part earns its spot.

1. Order and customer basics

Start with the essentials:

  • Order number: even a simple sequence (001, 002…) makes it easy to find records later.
  • Order date: when the customer placed the order.
  • Customer name, phone, and email: collect two ways to reach them in case of last-minute questions.
  • Event type and date: birthday, wedding, baby shower, office party. The event shapes design choices and how many servings are needed.

2. Pickup or delivery date and time

Write the exact pickup or delivery date and time, separate from the event date. Cakes are often picked up the day before or the morning of. Ambiguity here is one of the most common sources of stress.

If you deliver, record:

  • The delivery address and any access notes (venue contact, loading area, stairs)
  • A delivery fee
  • Whether setup is included

If the customer picks up, consider noting how the cake should be transported. A flat surface in the car and air conditioning on a hot day are worth reminding people about.

3. Cake specifications

This is the heart of the form:

  • Servings or size: the most important number for pricing. Ask how many guests, then agree on a serving count and size together. Different bakers use different slice-size assumptions, so be clear about yours.
  • Shape: round, square, sheet, or something else.
  • Number of tiers
  • Cake flavor, with a different flavor per tier if applicable
  • Filling
  • Frosting or finish: buttercream, fondant, ganache, naked or semi-naked
  • Colors and theme
  • Message or lettering: write the exact spelling, and repeat names back to the customer to confirm.

Using checkboxes for common options (shapes, frosting types) speeds up filling out the form and reduces handwriting mistakes.

4. Design notes and a sketch box

Leave a blank area for a quick sketch or notes about inspiration photos. If a customer sends a reference image, note which elements they actually want: the color palette, the drip, the flowers, the topper. Set expectations that a custom cake will be inspired by the reference rather than an exact copy, especially for designs from other bakers. Recording these decisions helps avoid "that's not what I pictured" moments.

5. Allergies and dietary needs

Always ask about allergies and dietary restrictions, and write the answer down even if it's "none." Common considerations include nuts, dairy, eggs, wheat or gluten, soy, and sesame. Be honest about your kitchen. If you can't guarantee an allergen-free environment, say so clearly on the form. Many home bakers include a line stating that products are made in a kitchen that also handles common allergens. Check what your local cottage food rules require you to disclose.

6. Pricing breakdown

Itemize the price instead of writing one number:

  • Base price: based on servings and standard design
  • Add-ons: premium flavors or fillings, fondant, sugar flowers, toppers, extra tiers, complex decorating
  • Delivery or setup fee
  • Cake stand or board deposit, if you lend reusable items
  • Total

Itemizing helps customers understand the price and makes it easy to adjust if they change something.

7. Deposit and payment terms

Most custom bakers take a deposit to reserve the date. Record:

  • Deposit amount and date paid
  • Payment method (cash, card, payment app)
  • Remaining balance and when it's due, such as at pickup or a set number of days before the event
  • A checkbox for "paid in full"

The deposit protects you from no-shows and from buying ingredients for an order that gets cancelled.

8. Policies

A short policy section avoids awkward conversations later. Common items:

  • Cancellation and refunds: for example, deposits are non-refundable within a certain number of days of the event.
  • Change deadline: the last date design or flavor changes can be accepted.
  • Responsibility after pickup: once a cake leaves your care, transport and storage are the customer's responsibility.
  • Returned items: the deadline for returning borrowed stands or boards.

Keep the wording simple and fair. Have the customer sign or initial the form, or confirm in writing by email or text, so you both agree on the details. If you have questions about legal enforceability where you live, a local small-business resource can help.

9. Two copies: customer and baker

Give the customer a copy, or a photo or PDF of the completed form, and keep your own. Your copy can include internal notes such as baking schedule, special supplies to buy, and decorating time estimates.

Paper, PDF, or online form?

There's no single right answer:

  • Printed forms work well for in-person consultations and markets.
  • Fillable PDFs or digital notes are easy to email.
  • Online forms can collect requests before you quote.

Whatever you use, keep the same fields every time. Consistency is what prevents mistakes.

After the order: use the form as your work plan

The order form is also your production plan. Use it to:

  • Schedule baking, cooling, filling, crumb coating, and decorating days
  • Build a shopping list
  • Confirm details with the customer a few days before pickup
  • Check everything (message spelling, colors, flavors) before handing over the cake
