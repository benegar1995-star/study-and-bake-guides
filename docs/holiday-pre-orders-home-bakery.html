How to Take Holiday Pre-Orders for a Home Bakery (Without Overselling)

Holidays can be the busiest weeks of the year for a home bakery. Thanksgiving pies, Christmas cookie boxes and Easter breads sell themselves when people already know you. But taking orders by text, DM and phone with no system is how bakers end up with 60 pies due on a day they can bake 40, or a customer standing at the door for an order nobody wrote down.

Pre-orders fix that. You decide in advance what you'll make, how much, and when people pick up. Here's how to set it up.

Step 1: Check the rules and your capacity

If you bake under your state's cottage food law, confirm that the items you plan to sell are allowed and labeled the way your state requires. Some states also limit annual sales or how products are delivered. Our cottage food label guide covers labels.

Then be honest about capacity. How many pies fit in your oven at once? How long does each batch take, including cooling? How many hours can you bake on each of the last two or three days? Work out a realistic maximum per item, then take off 10-15% for burned batches, a slow mixer, or a sick day.

Step 2: Keep the menu short

A holiday menu of 4-6 items is plenty. Pick things that:

  • Use overlapping ingredients and the same oven temperature
  • Hold well for a day or two (pies, breads and cookies beat delicate whipped cream cakes)
  • Come in fixed sizes, such as a 9-inch pie, a dozen rolls or a 6-pack, so pricing and planning stay simple

Price holiday items on full cost, not habit. Ingredients like butter, pecans and good chocolate cost more in November. Our guide to pricing baked goods walks through the math.

Step 3: Set caps, a cutoff and pickup slots

Before you announce anything, decide:

  • A cap for each item. When it's gone, it's gone. Say "limited quantities" in your post; it's true and it encourages early orders.
  • An order cutoff date. Usually 3-5 days before the holiday, so you can shop and plan. For Thanksgiving, that might be the Friday before.
  • Pickup days and time slots. One-hour slots (for example, 10-11 am) spread people out so your kitchen doesn't turn into a waiting room. Many bakers offer two pickup days, such as the Tuesday and Wednesday before Thanksgiving.

Step 4: Take orders in one place

Choose one way to take orders, such as a simple online form, a message template or a sheet you fill in during calls. Whatever comes in, log it in the same list right away. For every order, record:

  • Order number, customer name and phone or email
  • Pickup date and time slot
  • Each item and quantity (one line per item makes the totals easy)
  • Price, amount paid and balance due
  • Notes such as allergies, "no nuts" or a gift message

Ask for payment or a deposit when the order is placed. A deposit of 50% or full prepayment cuts no-shows sharply. State your policy clearly: what happens if someone cancels, and how long you'll hold an order after the pickup window.

Step 5: Turn orders into a bake plan

Once the cutoff passes (or each evening while orders come in), total up each item by pickup date. Divide by how many units you get per batch and round up. That's your bake list:

  • Pumpkin pie: 12 for Tuesday + 20 for Wednesday = 32 pies. At 4 per batch, that's 8 batches.
  • Dinner rolls (dozen): 6 + 10 = 16 dozen. At 3 dozen per batch, that's 6 batches (5.3, rounded up).

From there, work backward. Some doughs and pie crusts can be made and chilled or frozen days ahead. Fillings bake closer to pickup. Make a shopping list from your batch counts and buy the expensive ingredients right after the cutoff, when you know the real numbers.

Step 6: Run pickup day like a checklist

The night before, print a pickup list sorted by time slot. For each slot, have the orders boxed, labeled with the customer's name, and lined up in order. On pickup day:

  • Check off each order as it leaves
  • Collect any balance due (have your card reader charged and a little change ready)
  • Note anyone who didn't show, and follow your posted policy

A short "thank you, here's how to order for Christmas" card in each box is an easy way to get repeat customers.

Step 7: Review while it's fresh

Within a few days, write down what sold out, what didn't, how many hours it really took, and your profit after ingredients and packaging. Add the totals to your regular books (our bookkeeping guide explains a simple routine). Next season, you can set caps based on real numbers instead of hope.
